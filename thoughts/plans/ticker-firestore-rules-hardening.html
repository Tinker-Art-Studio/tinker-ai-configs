Goal: the database refuses what the app's screens never let a staff member do. Today the screens enforce the limits and the rules don't, so anyone signed in can skip the screens.

How it works today

Every staff app talks to Firestore straight from the browser. The app's screens decide what a staff member is shown how to do. The rules decide what the database accepts. A signed-in person can skip the screens and write to the database directly with their own login.

Exactly how it could happen

No hacking tools or special skills, only a signed-in browser.

Path 1: a staff member misusing their own login

  1. They sign in to Ticker normally, on a laptop with any desktop browser.
  2. They open the browser's developer console: it's built into every desktop browser, one keyboard shortcut away (⌥⌘J in Chrome on a Mac), and needs no installs or admin rights.
  3. The app's own database connection is right there. Ticker loads Firebase as a plain browser script (the firebase global from the compat SDK), so the same object the app saves with is reachable from the console, already signed in as that person.
  4. They type a single line in the form "in clock entries, document X, set field Y to Z". It's the same kind of save the app does when you tap a button, just with values the app would never send. Finding the document's id is easy, because the app loads their own entries to display them.
  5. Firestore checks the rules, not the app. The rules only ask "is this their own entry?", the answer is yes, and the write is accepted, saved, and shown to managers like any other entry.

What that one line could do today:

  • move their own clock-in earlier, or clock-out later, in the current pay period
  • mark their own time-correction request "approved"
  • set their own time-off request to "approved", or mark their sub "confirmed"
  • for the roster, rewrite the whole list for everyone

What they can't do, even today:

  • touch anyone else's clock entries or time-off
  • change their own role or app access
  • edit schedules, pay-period locks, or other managers' data

It's a few minutes of effort for someone who has used a browser console before, or who follows a short how-to for Firebase apps. It isn't something anyone does by accident.

Would anyone notice? Probably not right away. The edited entry looks like any other, and nothing in Ticker records who last changed it or what it was before. Firestore doesn't keep a change history by default.

Path 2: a stolen login

Someone else gets into a staff account and has exactly the same powers as that staff member. The realistic ways:

  • The exposed user-import.html passwords. The starting passwords for 9 named staff were on a public page until 65e3c41 stopped publishing it (Sep 30). Anyone who never changed theirs still has a login that could have been read. This is a prerequisite, separate from this plan: reset those passwords, or confirm each was changed.
  • A shared or studio computer left signed in.
  • A reused password leaked from another site, or a phishing email.

A stranger with a Ticker login could also rewrite or wipe the roster for everyone. In the app that would look like "the data disappeared" for the whole studio.

Why the risk is low, and why it's still worth doing

It needs either a staff member willing to falsify their own records, which is a trust and HR issue more than a technical one, or a compromised account. Every change is limited to that person's own records, except the roster.

But Ticker's clock entries are payroll's source of truth, approvals should mean what they say, and today it can happen with only a browser and nothing would flag it.

What would change (direction, not yet designed)

DataAfter
Clock entriesA staff member's update can touch only the correction request (diff(resource.data).affectedKeys().hasOnly(['changeRequest'])), only as a new pending request, and can't change the uid. Times, hours and approval stay manager-only. Creates are limited to known entry types and their own uid. Consider a createdAt == request.time stamp for an audit trail, since timestamps are client-generated by design.
Time offA staff member's update is limited to the fields the edit, withdraw and comment screens use. Status can only move to withdrawn, coverage confirmation stays manager-only, and the uid is pinned. This turns the sub-confirm guarantee into a real one.
RosterStaff can no longer write the roster doc directly. Rules can't compare a list entry by entry, so the claim needs a new route. Either (a) a small server function that verifies the caller and checks "this entry is unclaimed and it's you" before writing, or (b) split the roster into one doc per person, which is a data migration and falls under the Removing a data source rule. This is the biggest design decision. Option (a) also changes Ticker's code, so it needs Ticker approval.
StreaksType and range checks only (numbers, non-negative). Streaks are a game, not pay.
KioskIts broad rights are by design (shared device, PIN-based). Check whether its entry creates and streak writes can be narrowed the same way without breaking kiosk clock-in/out.

Staff shouldn't notice any difference in normal use. Every button keeps working, and only writes the app never makes are refused.

Suggested split

  1. Clock entries + time off: rules-only, and closes the payroll gap first.
  2. Roster claim: rules plus a Ticker code change, with its own design decision.
  3. Streaks (and the kiosk, if worthwhile).
